# 🧠 Think Like a Problem Solver, Not a Programmer

*Session Date: 30 June 2026*

---

## 1. The Big Shift

> "We are not going to become software engineers anymore — AI has already covered that."

The role of a developer has evolved. Writing code is no longer the hard part — AI tools, freelancers, and affordable subscriptions can do that. The real value today lies in **identifying the right problem** and **solving it in a way people are willing to pay for**.

Think of it like a restaurant:
- A cook makes Pani Puri.
- An **owner** solves problems — more customers, better revenue, smoother operations.

**Your goal: become the owner, not the cook.**

---

## 2. Before Writing a Single Line of Code

These three questions must be answered **before** any coding begins:

### ✅ Market Research
- Talk to at least **20–30 people** in your target audience.
- Ask: *"If I build this, will you pay for it?"*
- If people are not willing to pay, the idea is not viable — no matter how good it sounds.

### ✅ Trust & Retention
- Even if you onboard users, they may stop using your app.
- Ask: *"Why would users keep coming back?"*
- Retention (bringing users back repeatedly) is as important as acquisition.

### ✅ Revenue Model
- Know how you will make money **before** building.
- What feature will users pay for? How much? Per use, per month, per lead?
- Without a revenue model, you are doing charity — and charity doesn't pay your bills.

### ✅ Vibe Coding is a scam
- https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pQd8stK3nnw
- Never do vibe coding or hackathons without a clear revenue model. 
- You will waste time building something nobody will pay for.
- Build a product that solves a real problem, not just something that looks cool.

## 3. Bad Idea Examples

### ❌ "Nearby Services App" (within 1–2 km radius)
Sounds exciting — but **Google Maps already does this**, and it does it better. Businesses trust Google. Building a competitor means earning that trust from scratch, which takes years. The problem is already solved.

### ❌ "Uber for Ferries"
Ferries run on **fixed schedules**, not on-demand. They already have websites with live updates. Nobody books a ferry the way they book an Uber. The problem doesn't exist.

**Lesson:** If the problem you are solving doesn't exist, nobody will use your solution — no matter how well you build it.

---

## 4. Good Idea Example: Spot My Guru

### The Problem
Individual tutors — home tuition teachers, yoga instructors, personal gym trainers — **cannot list themselves on Google Maps** because Google requires business registration (GST number, PAN, office address). These individuals teach from home or visit clients, but they have no online presence.

### The Solution
An app that lets:
- **Gurus** (teachers, trainers, yoga instructors) list their services.
- **Students** search for nearby coaching by subject or class level.

### The Revenue Model
- Students browse for **free**.
- Gurus pay **per lead** (e.g., ₹10 per student contact they receive).

### The Math (Scale it up)
| Scale | Leads/Month | Revenue/Teacher | Teachers | Total Revenue |
|-------|------------|-----------------|----------|---------------|
| 1 teacher | 100 leads × ₹10 | ₹1,000 | 1 | ₹1,000 |
| 1 society | 100 leads × ₹10 | ₹1,000 | 10 | ₹10,000 |
| 1 city | 100 leads × ₹10 | ₹1,000 | 10,000 | ₹1 Crore/month |

**This is how you think about scale.** Start small. Validate. Then expand.

> Note: This problem has already been solved by Spot My Guru. The point is to learn *how* to think, not to replicate the idea.

## 5. Avoid the "Me Too" Trap

Entering a market that is **already dominated** is called a **"Me Too"** approach.

Examples: Blinkit, Zomato, Swiggy — the food delivery space is full. If you build another food delivery app, you are fighting for a tiny slice of a market where users already have 3+ strong alternatives.

**Always look for a niche** — a specific problem that existing big players have overlooked or cannot solve due to their size or structure.

---

## 6. Fail Early

> "Set a deadline. If the idea doesn't show momentum by then — leave it."

- Maximum time for brainstorming + research + finding paying customers: **be defined upfront**.
- If after serious effort there is **no revenue, no adoption, no momentum** — it is okay to stop.
- Move to the next idea. There are infinite problems in the world to solve.
- Failing early saves years of time, money, and energy.

**Exception:** Long-term R&D projects (e.g., building a rocket) have different timelines — calibrate your deadline to the nature of the idea.

---

## 7. Don't Fall in Love With Your Product

> "Be professional. Keep some distance."

- It is easy to keep building features for an app you love — even when nobody is using it.
- If a product is not generating value (users, revenue, impact), **let it go**.
- Your time, energy, and health are finite resources. Invest them wisely.
- Have multiple ideas. Never be emotionally attached to just one.

---

## 8. Key Takeaways

| # | Principle | What It Means |
|---|-----------|---------------|
| 1 | **Be a problem solver** | Think like a business owner, not a coder |
| 2 | **Market research first** | Talk to 20–30 people before writing code |
| 3 | **Revenue model matters** | Know how you will make money upfront |
| 4 | **Niche over "Me Too"** | Find overlooked problems, not crowded spaces |
| 5 | **Retain users** | Getting users is hard; keeping them is harder |
| 6 | **Fail early** | Set a deadline; pivot if there is no traction |
| 7 | **Stay detached** | Be professional; don't fall in love with your product |

## 💬 Discussion Questions for Students

1. Think of an app idea you have had. Did you do market research before thinking of building it?
2. Can you find a problem in your own community that is **not** solved by Google or any major app?
3. For your idea — who are the 20–30 people you would talk to first?
4. What is your revenue model? Who pays, and for what?
